# 1. How to use this file

This file is a reference, not a single recipe. It is organised from principles (sections 3–4) through evidence (section 5) to each stage of the process (sections 6–16), and ends with concrete recipes (section 17) and printable checklists (section 18).

If you only want to bake, jump to **section 17**. If something goes wrong, go to **section 16**. If you want to know *why* a number is what it is, the relevant stage section explains it and points back to the recipes that support it.

Three ideas run through everything:

1. **Yeast, time and temperature are one system.** A yeast number means nothing without the other two. The compilation's yeast doses swing about 25× and almost all of them are correct for their schedule.
2. **The fridge decouples maturation from leavening.** Cold slows the yeast far more than it slows the enzymes, so flavour and extensibility keep developing while gas production nearly stops.
3. **Judge the dough, not the clock.** Every serious source, from the AVPN rules to home bloggers, gives readiness signs (smooth, elastic, doubled, poke fills back slowly) that override the times.

# 3. Core concepts

## 3.1 Baker's percentages

Every ingredient is expressed as a percentage of the **flour** weight, which is always 100%.

| Term | Meaning | Example per 1 kg flour |
|---|---|---|
| 65% hydration | water = 65% of flour | 650 g water |
| 2.8% salt | salt = 2.8% of flour | 28 g salt |
| 0.2% fresh yeast | yeast = 0.2% of flour | 2 g fresh yeast |
| 0.1% instant yeast | yeast = 0.1% of flour | 1 g instant dry yeast |

Why flour is the anchor: it is the ingredient that defines the dough's structure. Water, salt and yeast are all read against it. Recipes written against a fixed amount of *water* (like the AVPN and STG rules, which start from 1 litre) have to be re-based onto flour before they can be compared.

## 3.2 Dough factor and scaling

To build any recipe from a target number of balls:

1. Total dough = number of balls × ball weight.
2. Dough factor = 1 + hydration + salt + yeast (+ oil if used), all as decimals.
3. Flour = total dough ÷ dough factor.
4. Every other ingredient = flour × its percentage.

Worked example (12 balls × 270 g at 66% water, 2.8% salt, 0.3% fresh yeast):

| Step | Calculation | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Total dough | 12 × 270 | 3,240 g |
| Dough factor | 1 + 0.66 + 0.028 + 0.003 | 1.691 |
| Flour | 3,240 ÷ 1.691 | ~1,916 g → round to 1,920 g |
| Water | 1,920 × 0.66 | ~1,267 g |
| Salt | 1,920 × 0.028 | ~54 g |
| Fresh yeast | 1,920 × 0.003 | ~5.8 g |
| Or instant yeast | 1,920 × 0.001 | ~1.9 g |

Add about 1–2% extra dough to allow for what sticks to the bowl and hands.

## 3.3 Leavening vs maturation

Italian sources treat these as two different processes, and the distinction explains most scheduling decisions.

**Leavening (*lievitazione*)** is what yeast does: it eats sugars and produces CO₂ and alcohol, inflating the dough. It is fast and very temperature-sensitive. Too much and the dough over-proofs: the gluten is stretched past its limit, bubbles merge and collapse, and the dough slackens and tears.

**Maturation (*maturazione*)** is what enzymes and time do. Amylases break starch into simpler sugars (food for the yeast later, and browning in the oven). Proteases relax the gluten, making it extensible. Flavour compounds accumulate. Italian recipes most often cite **digestibility** as the payoff.

**Why the fridge matters:** at about 4 °C the yeast nearly stops while the enzymes keep working slowly. So a dough can mature for 24–48 hours and still have its leavening "in reserve" for the final warm proof and the oven spring. This is why long cold recipes use relatively little yeast — but, as section 9 shows, not *too* little, because the yeast still has to do all the leavening in a few warm hours at the end.

The trap: if the fridge runs too cold (2–3 °C), leavening is suppressed even more than the recipe assumes. The dough comes out under-fermented and needs a longer, warmer final proof.

# 4. The official rules

Two bodies define "Neapolitan" pizza: the EU STG specification (a legally registered Traditional Speciality Guaranteed) and the AVPN (Associazione Verace Pizza Napoletana), whose disciplinare is revised periodically. They agree on almost everything; the AVPN version is slightly broader.

## 4.1 STG "Pizza Napoletana" (EU specification)

Based on the STG specification text (Art. 3), as published in a 2022 PDF from Horeca News:

| Parameter | STG rule |
|---|---|
| Water | 1 litre, into the mixer first |
| Salt | 50–55 g per litre, dissolved in the water |
| Yeast | 3 g brewer's yeast per litre, dissolved in the water |
| Flour | 10% added first, then ~1,800 g in total, W 220–380 |
| Water absorption | Flour should absorb about 50–55% of its weight in water |
| Mixing | ~10 min to the *punto di pasta*, then ~20 min on low speed, preferably with a fork mixer |
| Dough temperature | Fermentation at about 25 °C (±10%); avoid overheating while mixing |
| Puntata | 2 h on the bench, covered with a damp cloth so no crust forms |
| Staglio | Cut with a spatula, shaped by hand, 180–250 g balls |
| Appretto | 4–6 h in boxes at room temperature; use within 6 h after that |
| Opening | By hand only, from the centre outward; no rolling pin, no press |
| Diameter | Max 35 cm |
| Rim (cornicione) | 1–2 cm high |
| Centre thickness | Max 0.4 cm (+10%) |
| Oven | Wood-fired; hearth ~485 °C, vault ~430 °C |
| Bake | 60–90 seconds; dough core reaches 60–65 °C |

Normalised per 1 kg flour, the STG dough is roughly: water ~555 g (55.5%), salt ~29 g (2.9%), fresh yeast ~1.7 g (0.17%).

## 4.2 AVPN disciplinare (association rules, 2022 revision)

From the research done earlier in this project (the AVPN disciplinare PDF):

| Parameter | AVPN 2022 range |
|---|---|
| Salt | 40–60 g per litre of water |
| Fresh yeast | 0.1–3 g per litre of water |
| Flour | 1.6–1.8 kg per litre of water, type 00 or 0 (W 250–320 per the compilation's reading) |
| Total fermentation | 8–24 h |
| Kneading | 10–20 min (compilation #1) |
| Dry:fresh yeast | 1:3 |
| Balls | 180–250 g |
| Bake | 430–480 °C, 60–90 s |

Two things are worth noticing:

- **The 2022 range widened the yeast floor to 0.1 g per litre** and the total time to 24 h. That officially legitimises low-yeast, long-room-temperature doughs.
- **Hydration is implied, not stated.** 1 litre per 1.6–1.8 kg flour = 56–63% hydration. Canotto doughs at 65–78% sit outside the classic rules; that is why contemporary pizza is judged separately.

The compilation's summary of AVPN (#1) gives 58% hydration, 3.0% salt and 0.17% fresh yeast, with a 2 h bulk and 4–6 h appretto. Its own total time contradicts itself in three places (8–24 h, 8–12 h, 6–8 h) — see section 5.5.

## 4.3 Canotto / contemporary pizza "rules"

There is no official canotto standard. Scatti di Gusto (2019) proposed a ten-rule "manifesto" based on observation, which has become the usual reference:

| Parameter | Proposed canotto rule |
|---|---|
| Hydration | 65–78% |
| Ball weight | ~260–270 g |
| Diameter | Max 31 cm |
| Fermentation | Not fixed; direct or preferment; fresh, dry, sourdough or *pasta di riporto* all allowed; controlled temperature/humidity allowed |
| Flour | 00, 0, 1 or 2, alone or blended in any proportion; soft wheat only; any additives must be declared |
| Rim | Very pronounced, built from large alveoli |

The defining feature is the rim — the article's phrase is in section 19. Everything else is permissive. The practical takeaway: **a canotto is mostly made by higher hydration, a well-risen ball, and an opening technique that never presses the rim**, not by any one secret ingredient.

A Scatti di Gusto home-oven canotto recipe takes a different route: 00 flour at roughly 67–71% hydration, with sugar and oil (to help browning at 250–280 °C), about 10 minutes of vigorous hand kneading, 30 minutes' rest, 230 g balls, then 7–8 hours of proofing in a cool place. Its opening instruction is the important part: press from the centre outward so the gas is pushed into the rim, then widen without flattening the edge.

## 5.2 What the 20 agree on

- **Four ingredients.** Flour, water, salt, yeast. Only #12 (oil), #15 and #20 (sugar, oil) and the home-oven canotto add anything, and those additions are for home ovens at 250 °C, not for 450 °C.
- **Salt is tight.** Most land at 2.4–3.2%. The classic window is 2.5–3%.
- **Yeast varies enormously, and correctly.** From 0.021% to 0.50% fresh-equivalent. The driver is always temperature × time.
- **Long ferments happen in bulk, in the fridge.** The 24–48 h recipes almost all mature as one mass in the fridge and ball 3–6 h before baking.
- **The final proof decides the result.** Every method ends with balls rising at room temperature until roughly doubled and relaxed.
- **Long ferments need strong flour.** The 48 h recipes (#2, #3, #5) use W 280+ or stronger blends.

## 5.3 Where they split

**Hydration** forms two camps:

| Camp | Range | Recipes | Character |
|---|---|---|---|
| Traditional / verace | 58–65% | 1, 7, 9, 11, 12, 14, 17, 19, 20 | Easier to handle and launch; more burn-resistant at 480–500 °C; classic ruota di carro |
| Contemporary / high hydration | 68–78% | 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10, 13, 15, 16, 18 | More open, airy rim; stickier; chars faster |

**Fermentation systems** form three families:

| System | Recipes | Typical yeast (fresh eq.) | Shape of schedule |
|---|---|---|---|
| All room temperature, short (4–9 h) | 1, 11, 12 | 0.065–0.30% | Bulk 1–2 h → balls 2–8 h |
| All room temperature, long (20–25 h) | 8, 10 | 0.02–0.08% | Long warm bulk → balls ~5 h |
| Short warm start → long cold bulk → warm ball proof | 2, 3, 4, 5, 13, 15, 18 | 0.10–0.45% | 45 min–4 h TA → 18–48 h fridge → balls 3–6 h |
| Preferment | 14, 16, 17, 20 | 0.2–0.5% | Biga or poolish 15–24 h → short final dough |
| Cold-balled | 6, 17 | 0.2–0.24% | Bulk → balls rest in fridge → short warm-up |

The key lesson from this table: **do not borrow room-temperature hours from one family and paste them onto another.** A 24 h room-temperature dough uses about a tenth of the yeast of a cold 48 h dough; copying its warm hours onto a cold plan, or its yeast onto a warm plan, breaks the balance.

**Bulk-first vs ball-early:**

| Approach | Recipes | Why chosen | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bulk-first (puntata, then staglio late) | 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 12, 13, 15, 16, 17, 18, 20 | Builds strength evenly; one container in the fridge; ball when the bake time is known | Final proof must be timed well |
| Ball-early (staglio soon after mixing) | 7, 9, 10, 11 | Tradition; less handling, fewer large bubbles that scorch; very even proof | Needs fridge space for many boxes; little chance to add strength later |

**Kneading** splits into continuous kneading (AVPN 10–20 min; Vale ≥15 min by hand; Capuano ≥10 min by hand; Vitiello, Mariatta ~10 min in a mixer) and minimal-knead-plus-folds (#3 rest 1 h then 4 fold sets; #17 autolysis then 3 fold sets; #18 one minute of mixing then folds in the fridge).

## 5.4 Recipe-by-recipe digest

Each entry gives the formula per 1 kg flour, the method in brief, and what it teaches.

### #1 — AVPN disciplinare
- **Formula:** water ~580 g, salt ~30 g, fresh yeast ~1.7 g. Flour 00/0, W 250–320.
- **Method:** Dissolve salt in water at 20–22 °C; add 10% of the flour; dissolve yeast; add the rest of the flour gradually; knead 10–20 min to ~23 °C dough. Bulk 2 h; staglio 180–250 g; appretto 4–6 h; open from the centre on semola; bake 430–480 °C for 60–90 s.
- **Teaches:** the reference point. Low hydration, little yeast, all warm, same day.

### #2 — Le mille ricette di vale (48 h, Ooni)
- **Formula:** water 700 g, salt 24 g, dry yeast 2 g (≈ 6 g fresh at 1:3; the compilation lists 0.40% using the source's 1:2). Flour tipo 0.
- **Method:** By hand: flour + half the water + yeast, add the rest of the water gradually, then salt; **knead at least 15 min**. Rest 30 min, letter-fold, rest 30 min, fold again, final tight pirlatura. Fridge 42 h. Divide into 270 g balls; proof 3–4 h (3 in summer, 4 in winter). Open on semola leaving a 1–1.5 cm border; Ooni ~420 °C, ~60 s.
- **Teaches:** the closest published match to a hand-kneaded 48 h canotto at 270 g.

### #3 — La Cucina Ignorante (48 h)
- **Formula:** water 700 g, salt 28 g, fresh yeast 2 g. Flour 700 g W 330 + 300 g W 400.
- **Method:** Yeast dissolved in all the water; 3/4 of it into the flour; rest of the water in sips with the salt. Rest 1 h. **4 fold sets, 15 min apart.** Fridge at 4 °C for 48 h, undisturbed. Out 10 min, cut (don't tear) 260–290 g pieces, light pirlatura, proof 3–5 h in narrow containers.
- **Teaches:** the strong-blend approach (70/30 with a W 400) for 48 h; minimal kneading replaced by folds.

### #4 — La Cucina Ignorante (24 h)
- **Formula:** water 700 g, salt 25 g, fresh yeast 2 g. Flour W 330.
- **Method:** Mixer; if the dough won't close at 70%, rest it 15 min in the fridge and continue. 4 h at room temperature, 24 h in the fridge at 4–6 °C. Out ~6 h before baking; 260 g balls rolled between the palms; proof 4–6 h until roughly doubled.
- **Teaches:** a shorter cold phase gets a longer warm start (4 h). Also: for a verace look, don't fold the balls, just close them, to avoid trapped bubbles that burn. Manitoba alone is discouraged for 16–24 h.

### #5 — Raffaele Vitiello (70%, 48 h)
- **Formula:** water 700 g, salt 27 g, fresh yeast 3 g in winter / 2 g in summer. Caputo rossa W ≥ 280.
- **Method:** Mix 5 min, add salt, work to a compact dough (≥10 min). Rest 45 min, a few fold rounds, fridge ≥40 h. Out ≥6 h before baking, sit 1 h, ball 250 g, proof 4–5 h (max 3 h in summer).
- **Teaches:** the seasonal yeast swap — a clean demonstration that temperature changes the dose.

### #6 — In Cucina con Mariatta (78%)
- **Formula:** water ~783 g, salt ~48 g (suspicious), fresh yeast ~2.4 g. Flour 0 + "croccante" blend.
- **Method:** Mixer; salt then knead ~10 min; add the remaining water gradually (the dough breaks and comes back). 1 h warm, ~35 h fridge, ball 250–300 g, fridge again ~10 h, out 2–3 h before. Ferrari fornetto ~400 °C, ~5 min.
- **Teaches:** the double cold rest; and a warning to sanity-check salt.

### #7 — Giovanni Brigandi (verace)
- **Formula:** water 650 g, salt 25 g, fresh yeast 1 g. Caputo Pizzeria rossa.
- **Method:** Dissolve yeast, add flour gradually (or flour-first reverse method). Minimal handling — avoid heavy folding so no big bubbles burn. Bulk, ball 275 g, proof until ready. Floor ~430 °C, dome ~490 °C, 60–90 s.
- **Teaches:** the verace preference for gentle handling; 65% as the classic upper limit; a contemporary variant at 70–85% baked cooler for ~120 s.

### #8 — Pizza Mon Amour (71%, Ooni)
- **Formula:** water ~709 g, salt ~32 g, fresh yeast ~0.76 g. Caputo Blu (= Pizzeria).
- **Method:** Water, dissolve yeast, salt; add flour gradually; mix ~15 min without overheating. 20 h covered in the switched-off oven, undisturbed. Gentle staglio of ~250 g; 5 h in an insulated box.
- **Teaches:** all-warm 25 h with tiny yeast. Notably uses Caputo Pizzeria for 25 h at room temperature — longer than the mill's 8–12 h rating.

### #9 — Spice / Caputo Nuvola (62%)
- **Formula:** water ~620 g, salt ~30 g, fresh yeast ~2.2 g. Caputo Nuvola.
- **Method:** Cold water, yeast, then salt; knead smooth; bulk; 280 g balls; proof; fornetto.
- **Teaches:** 62% holds its shape; yeast "give or take a gram" to the kitchen temperature.

### #10 — Spice "Diavola 16" (24 h warm)
- **Formula:** water ~682 g, salt ~27 g, fresh yeast ~0.21 g (0.021%).
- **Method:** Mix, tiny yeast, salt, knead; 24 h at room temperature; balls ~280 g; proof; bake.
- **Teaches:** the lowest yeast in the collection, possible only because every hour is warm. Tiny doses must be weighed via a solution.

### #11 — Gino Sorbillo (madia)
- **Formula:** water ~645 g, salt ~29 g, fresh yeast ~0.65 g. Flour 0, W 270–330, ≥12% protein.
- **Method:** Salt into the flour; 1 g yeast dissolved in 1 litre of water; mix by hand in the madia until it stops sticking to hands and walls; stretch and re-close on the bench. Bulk 1 h; 250 g balls shaped "like mozzarella"; appretto ~8 h until roughly doubled.
- **Teaches:** ball-early, all warm, hand-mixed — the most traditional workflow.

### #12 — Vincenzo Capuano (TV, direct, ~4 h)
- **Formula:** water 650 g (very cold), salt 25 g, fresh yeast 3 g, oil 20 g.
- **Method:** Flour + yeast + cold water by hand; salt and the rest of the water; when smooth add oil; **knead with force at least 10 min by hand.** Bulk 2 h; balls 270–290 g; proof ~2 h.
- **Teaches:** the short end — 0.30% yeast for a 4 h day. Oil adds extensibility for home ovens.

### #13 — Vincenzo Capuano (autolisi, 75%)
- **Formula:** water 750 g (550 + 200), salt 30 g, fresh yeast ~1 g. Flour W 300+.
- **Method:** Flour + 550 g water in the mixer 4–6 min; rest 30 min–24 h (stronger flour = longer); add yeast and run; add 200 g water slowly; salt; knead 7–8 min; rest 5 min. Fridge 18 h; balls; double in ~6 h at 20 °C.
- **Teaches:** autolysis and two-stage water (bassinage) are what make 75% manageable.

### #14 — Capuano "napolitudine" (biga)
- **Formula (combined):** water ~600 g, salt ~17.5 g, fresh yeast ~5 g. Manitoba (biga) + tipo 0.
- **Method:** Biga of Manitoba, cold water and yeast, fridge 24 h; final dough with tipo 0, salt, water; balls; 2 h proof; 250 °C, partly fried.
- **Teaches:** where Manitoba belongs on its own — in a preferment.

### #15 — Sorbillo-style 24 h (cosacucinooggi)
- **Formula:** water ~700 g, salt ~25 g, fresh yeast ~1 g, pinch of sugar. Strong 0.
- **Method:** Aerate flour; yeast + sugar in water; half the water then the rest; salt with the mixer running; folds; 2 h warm; ~20 h fridge; ~250 g balls; warm proof.
- **Teaches:** the "2 h warm then fridge" pattern; sugar as insurance for a tiny dose.

### #16 — AcasaconGiulia (poolish)
- **Formula (combined):** water ~697 g, salt ~24 g, fresh yeast ~2.7 g. W 250–310.
- **Method:** Poolish (420 g flour, 420 g water, 1.5 g yeast) 16 h at 20–22 °C; final dough with 2 g more yeast; salt and remaining water; keep dough below ~25 °C; rest 30 min; 3 fold rounds; fridge 2 h; balls 200–280 g; proof ~2 h; 430–480 °C, 60–90 s; centre ~0.25 cm.
- **Teaches:** the preferment route to aroma on a short final schedule.

### #17 — The Wispy Cloud (lievito madre / poolish)
- **Formula:** water ~650 g, salt ~25 g, leavening by sourdough or a small poolish. Strong flour ≥12% protein.
- **Method:** Autolysis 1 h; add leaven, oil, then salt; rest 30 min; 3 fold sets 10 min apart; bulk to near-double at ~26 °C; 250 g balls; 30 min warm then ≥18 h in the fridge; 30 min out while the oven heats.
- **Teaches:** the cold-balled hybrid; strong flour for long ferments.

### #18 — Dieta Mediterranea Picetto (hand, fridge)
- **Formula:** water 700 g, salt 20 g, dry yeast 1.5 g (= 0.15% dry, ≈ 0.45% fresh). 50/50 00 + 0.
- **Method:** Salt and dry yeast mixed into the flour; all the water at once; ~1 min with a spatula to a shaggy mass; fridge 40 min; folds during an 8–24 h fridge maturation; balls 240–250 g; proof.
- **Teaches:** the "no-knead" end of the spectrum; and that salt touching yeast in the flour is a myth worth ignoring.

### #19 — Sfida Pizza (Napoletana DOC)
- **Formula:** water ~600 g, salt ~30 g, fresh yeast ~0.5 g ("symbolic"). Dedicated strong pizza flour.
- **Method:** Weigh the tiny dose via a solution (1 g dry yeast in 30 g of 35–40 °C water with ½ tsp sugar, take a fraction); flour gradually, then salt; long bulk 24 h+; ~250 g balls.
- **Teaches:** symbolic yeast + long time = digestibility and aroma; the solution method for tiny doses.

### #20 — Nonna Virgi (poolish)
- **Formula (combined):** water ~635 g, salt ~2%, fresh yeast ~4.7 g. Tipo 0.
- **Method:** Poolish 500 g flour + 500 ml warm water + 2 g yeast + 1 tsp sugar, 15 h; final dough with 350 g flour, 40 ml water, 2 g yeast, salt, oil; short bulk; proof; home oven.
- **Teaches:** a simple everyday poolish dough.

# 6. Flour

## 6.1 What W, P/L and "00" mean

- **W** (strength) comes from the Chopin alveograph: a bubble of dough is inflated until it bursts, and W is the energy needed. In practice it tells you **how long the dough can ferment** before the gluten degrades.
- **P/L** is the ratio of tenacity (P, resistance) to extensibility (L). Around 0.5–0.6 is balanced for pizza: it stretches without snapping back too hard.
- **00 / 0 / 1 / 2** describe how finely the flour is milled and how much bran remains (00 is the most refined). They say **nothing about strength**. A 00 can be weak or strong.
- **Protein %** is a rough proxy when W isn't printed: ≥12% for 24 h, ≥13% for 48 h.

## 6.2 Matching W to fermentation time

| W | Protein (approx.) | Suits | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| 170–220 | 9–10.5% | 2–6 h | Generic pastry 00 |
| 220–260 | 11–12% | 6–12 h, same-day pizza | Some Caputo Pizzeria lots, 5 Stagioni |
| 260–300 | 12–13% | 12–24 h | Caputo Pizzeria, Caputo Nuvola |
| 300–340 | 13–14.5% | 24–48 h cold | Caputo Nuvola Super, Caputo Cuoco, Manitoba blends |
| 340–400 | 14.5%+ | 48–72 h, preferments | Manitoba, reinforcing flours |

The compilation's 48 h recipes all use W 280+ (#2 strong 0, #3 W 330+400, #5 W ≥280). That is not a coincidence: a weak flour cannot survive two days of enzyme and yeast activity; it turns slack and unworkable.

## 6.3 The three flours in this project

**Caputo Pizzeria (blue bag).** Mulino Caputo lists it at W 260–280 with P/L 0.50–0.60, made for 8–12 hours of medium leavening and real Neapolitan pizza at high temperature. It's the standard Naples pizzeria flour. For 48 h cold it is near its limit, though cold slows the enzymes enough that home bakers do push it to 24–48 h.

**Caputo Nuvola.** Also W 260–280 on the mill's page, but sold for contemporary, high-rim, highly hydrated doughs with 24 h or more of rising. It's the natural canotto flour. (The compilation lists it as both "W 300+" and "W 260–300"; the mill's own figure is 260–280.) Kept aside in this project for a special occasion.

**K Plus Manitoba (W ~380).** Very strong, high protein. Alone, it is too tenacious for a short ferment: the dough fights back when opened and shrinks. It belongs in a blend or a preferment (#3 uses W 400 at 30%; #14 uses Manitoba in the biga). La Cucina Ignorante explicitly discourages Manitoba alone for 16–24 h.

## 6.4 Blending

The effective W of a blend is roughly the weighted average of the two (not exact, but close enough for planning):

| Blend | Approx. W | Use |
|---|---|---|
| 100% Caputo Pizzeria | 260–280 | Same day to 24 h |
| 85% Pizzeria + 15% Manitoba | ~280–295 | 12–24 h; a same-day dough tolerates this much |
| 75% Pizzeria + 25% Manitoba | ~290–305 | 36–48 h cold (the party dough) |
| 70% Pizzeria + 30% Manitoba | ~295–310 | Upper limit; dough gets tight and springy |
| 100% Manitoba | ~380 | Preferments only |

Rules that came out of this project:

- **For 48 h cold, 25% Manitoba** strengthens Caputo Pizzeria enough to hold structure.
- **Don't exceed ~30%** in the final dough, or it becomes tight, springy and hard to open into a big rim.
- **For a same-day dough, 0–15% Manitoba.** A strong flour needs time to relax; in 6 h it stays tight.
- **Whisk the flours together dry** before any water goes in, so the blend is even.
- **Raise hydration slightly with more Manitoba** (stronger flour absorbs more water): 66% for the 75/25 blend instead of 65%.

# 7. Hydration

## 7.1 The ladder

| Hydration | Recipes | Handling in a 450–500 °C oven |
|---|---|---|
| 55–58% | STG, AVPN (#1) | Easiest to launch and turn; very burn-resistant; tighter rim |
| 60–63% | #14, #19, #9, #20 | Easy; classic verace feel |
| 65% | #7, #11, #12, #17 | Sweet spot: airy yet manageable |
| 66% | (the party dough) | Canotto entry point with a 25% Manitoba blend |
| 68% | #10 | Slightly sticky; use semola |
| 70–71% | #2, #3, #4, #5, #8, #15, #16, #18 | Wet; gentle handling; watch for char |
| 75% | #13 | Demanding; autolysis and two-stage water help |
| 78% | #6 | Most demanding |

## 7.2 How hydration interacts with the rest

- **Higher hydration → more open rim**, because a wetter dough expands more readily and steam inflates it in the oven.
- **Higher hydration → stickier**, harder to ball and open, and more likely to stick to the peel.
- **Higher hydration → rim chars before the crumb sets** at full 500 °C. That's why the bake temperature recommended here is 430–460 °C (section 15).
- **Stronger flour absorbs more water.** The same hydration feels drier with a strong blend.
- **Hand mixing gets harder above ~68%.** The canotto range starts at 65%, so 65–68% is the practical hand-mixed canotto zone.

## 7.3 Holding water back (bassinage)

Several recipes add the last part of the water late (#2: 320 + 30 ml; #3: 3/4 then sips; #13: 550 + 200 ml; #6: last water after kneading). The reason: gluten forms more easily in a slightly drier dough; once it has formed, it can absorb more water. In this project 30 g per bowl was held back and added with the salt.

**If the dough feels too sticky,** drop 2 points (e.g. 66% → 64%). That is a bigger change in feel than it sounds.

# 8. Salt

## 8.1 How much

- **Range:** 2.5–3.0% of flour weight in nearly every reliable recipe. 2.8% is a good default.
- **Per litre of water (STG):** 50–55 g. **AVPN 2022:** 40–60 g per litre.
- **Quick numbers:** 490 g flour → 14 g; 1 kg flour → 25–30 g; 1,920 g flour → 54 g.

## 8.2 What it does

- Flavour (a saltless pizza tastes flat).
- **Tightens the gluten**, making the dough stronger and less sticky.
- **Slows fermentation.** Salt draws water osmotically from yeast cells; it doesn't kill them at these concentrations, but it does slow them.
- Helps the crust colour.

## 8.3 When and how to add it — three methods

| Method | How | Who uses it | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dissolved in the water first | Salt into the water, then yeast, then flour gradually | STG, AVPN (#1), Pizza Mon Amour (#8) | Simple and even; the traditional Naples order |
| Mixed into the flour | Salt (and even dry yeast) stirred into the dry flour | Sorbillo (#11), Picetto (#18) | Picetto explicitly calls the "salt kills yeast" rule a myth |
| Added after the dough forms | Flour + water (+ yeast) first; salt later, dry or in reserved water | Vale (#2), Vitiello (#5), Capuano (#13), many mixer recipes | Lets gluten form before salt tightens it |

The method used in the party dough was a **composite**, not taken from any one recipe: mix flour, most of the water and the yeast; rest 20 minutes (autolysis-like, after Calvel); sprinkle the salt dry over the dough and squeeze it through (Forkish's "pincer" method); add the reserved 30 g water in splashes (bassinage).

Why the salt wasn't simply dissolved in the 30 g of reserved water: water dissolves only about 36 g of salt per 100 g, so 30 g of water holds about 11 g — less than half of the 27 g per bowl. Dry sprinkling plus pinching works.

Scatti di Gusto's guidance on salt is the same in spirit: add it as late as possible, either dry while the dough is still creamy or dissolved in reserved water.

# 9. Yeast

## 9.1 Types

| Type | Italian | Form | How to use | Keeps |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fresh (compressed) | Lievito di birra fresco | Moist beige cube (42 g in Croatia/Italy) | Crumble and dissolve in water | ~2 weeks in the fridge, wrapped; can be frozen |
| Active dry | Lievito secco attivo | Granules | Traditionally rehydrated in warm water first | Months unopened; weeks–months opened, cool and sealed |
| Instant dry | Lievito secco istantaneo | Fine granules (e.g. Dr. Oetker 7 g sachet) | Whisk straight into the flour | Months unopened, cool and dry |
| Sourdough | Lievito madre / li.co.li | Starter | Fed culture | Ongoing |

Naples tradition and the STG/AVPN rules use fresh yeast. Dry yeast is not inherently less reliable — but it is far more sensitive to how it was stored and to hot water, and a damaged packet looks identical to a good one.

## 9.2 Conversion

- **1 g dry (active or instant) ≈ 3 g fresh.** This is the AVPN ratio and the usual Italian rule.
- Some bakers use 1:2.5 or 1:2 (the compilation's #2 and #17 used 1:2). Pick one and stay consistent; this file uses 1:3 throughout.
- Instant is sometimes said to be slightly stronger than active dry; for pizza doses the difference disappears in rounding.

| Fresh | Dry/instant (1:3) |
|---|---|
| 1 g | 0.33 g |
| 2 g | 0.67 g |
| 3 g | 1.0 g |
| 6 g | 2.0 g |
| 21 g | 7 g (one sachet) |
| 42 g (one cube) | 14 g |

## 9.3 How much: dose vs time vs temperature

All figures are fresh-yeast equivalent as a % of flour, taken from the compilation and the official rules.

| Schedule | Temperature | Fresh eq. | Instant eq. | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ~4 h total | 22–25 °C | 0.30% | 0.10% | Capuano direct (#12) |
| 6–8 h total | ~25 °C | 0.17% | 0.06% | STG / AVPN (#1) |
| ~9 h total | room | 0.065% | 0.02% | Sorbillo (#11) |
| 20–25 h, all warm | 20–24 °C | 0.02–0.08% | 0.007–0.025% | Pizza Mon Amour (#8), Spice Diavola (#10) |
| 4 h warm + 24 h cold + 4–6 h warm | 4–6 °C cold | 0.20% | 0.07% | La Cucina Ignorante 24 h (#4) |
| 2 h warm + 20 h cold | ~4 °C | 0.10% (+sugar) | 0.03% | Sorbillo-style (#15) |
| 45 min warm + 40 h cold + 5 h warm | ~4 °C | 0.20–0.30% | 0.07–0.10% | Vitiello (#5) |
| 1 h warm + 48 h cold + 3–5 h warm | 4 °C | 0.20% | 0.07% | La Cucina Ignorante 48 h (#3) |
| 1.5 h warm + 42 h cold + 3–4 h warm | fridge | 0.40–0.60% | 0.13–0.20% | Vale (#2) |

Patterns:

- **Warm-only schedules need very little yeast.** Every warm hour is a full working hour.
- **Cold schedules need more than you'd think.** The fridge hours count for little, so the yeast has to do most of its work in the short warm windows at each end. The comparable 48 h cold recipes land at **0.20–0.40% fresh (≈ 0.07–0.13% instant)**.
- **Season matters.** Vitiello cuts yeast by a third in summer (#5); Vale adds an hour of proof in winter (#2).

**Lesson from this project:** 0.05% instant (0.15% fresh) for 48 h cold sits *below* every comparable recipe. It leaves no margin for a cold fridge or for yeast that has lost some strength. Aim for the middle of the range, about **0.30% fresh / 0.10% instant**, and adjust from your own results.

## 9.4 Weighing tiny doses accurately

Doses under 1 g are where errors creep in.

1. **Best: a 0.01 g jewellery scale.** Cheap, and it removes the problem.
2. **Fresh yeast is easier to weigh** than dry, because the same activity is three times the mass.
3. **Solution method (if no fine scale):** dissolve a known weight in a known weight of water, then take a proportional amount. Sfida Pizza (#19) uses 1 g dry yeast in 30 g warm water with ½ tsp sugar.
   - **Critical:** dry yeast does not truly dissolve; it settles to the bottom within minutes. **Stir thoroughly immediately before every portion**, and take portions quickly. Otherwise the first portions are mostly water and the last mostly yeast.
   - Use the solution within ~15–20 minutes.
4. **Instant yeast can go straight into the flour.** Weigh it, whisk it through the dry flour. This avoids both settling and hot-water risk.

## 9.5 Temperature and yeast

| Temperature | Yeast behaviour |
|---|---|
| Below ~4 °C | Nearly dormant; fermentation slows dramatically |
| 4–10 °C | Slow (fridge maturation zone) |
| 20–27 °C | Active; ideal for pizza dough (target dough ~23–25 °C) |
| 32–38 °C | Very active; fresh yeast proofing range (no hotter than ~38 °C) |
| 40–46 °C | Dry yeast activation range quoted by Bob's Red Mill (105–115 °F); upper edge of comfort |
| ~49 °C (120 °F) and up | Yeast starts dying |
| ~60 °C (140 °F) and up | Yeast killed |

A useful general rule (standard baking science rather than from a single source here): **fermentation speed roughly halves for every 8–10 °C drop.** So a dough at 2–3 °C ferments noticeably slower than one at 5 °C, and much slower than the recipe assumes if the recipe was written for 4–6 °C. A Fresh Loaf forum discussion puts it plainly: the fridge slows the yeast a lot but doesn't stop it, and activity drops sharply around 4–5 °C.

**Water temperature rule:** for pizza dough itself, use water at roughly 20–25 °C (cool to lukewarm). There is no need for warm water with instant or fresh yeast, and it removes any risk of overheating. If water feels warm on your wrist, it's above ~35 °C.

## 9.6 Testing yeast before you trust it

Do this whenever a packet has been open a while, travelled, or been stored anywhere warm — and always before a party-size batch.

**Dry or instant yeast:**
1. ½–1 tsp sugar into ~100 ml water at 32–40 °C (warm, not hot).
2. Stir in 1 tsp yeast (or the recipe amount).
3. Wait 10 minutes.

**Fresh yeast:** same, water no hotter than ~38 °C, about 10 minutes; it should foam and smell bready.

**Reading it:**

| Result | Meaning | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Thick foam within ~5 min (instant) or ~10 min (active dry / fresh) | Healthy | Use it |
| Foam only after 20–30 min | Alive but weak — a large share of cells dead | Don't dose by weight; replace, or use the activated mix and watch the dough |
| Nothing after 15 min | Dead | Discard |

Food Republic's guidance matches this: instant yeast foams within a few minutes, active dry in about ten, and no foam means the yeast has gone bad.

**Buying fresh yeast:** creamy beige, crumbly, smells pleasantly yeasty; no dry, dark, grey or slimy patches. Check the date.

## 9.7 How yeast fails (and the signs)

| Cause | Typical story | Sign in the dough |
|---|---|---|
| Too little for the schedule | Low dose + cold fridge | Slow rise; recovers with more warm time |
| Weak yeast (part-dead) | Packet aged or heated | Very slow; extra warm hours help only partly |
| Dead yeast | Hot water, heat in transit, old | No rise at all, even after a day warm; dense, gummy bake |
| Settled solution | Small portions taken from an unstirred solution | Unpredictable, often very weak rise |
| Too much | Doubled dose, warm kitchen | Balls over-proof early, slacken, tear, sour smell |

A dough that does **not** grow after many hours at room temperature is not "under-fermented" — it has a yeast problem. More time won't fix it; start again with tested yeast.

# 10. Mixing and kneading by hand

## 10.1 How long — what the sources actually say

| Source | Kneading | Mode |
|---|---|---|
| STG specification | ~10 min to come together + ~20 min slow | Mixer (fork) |
| AVPN (#1) | 10–20 min | Mixer or hand |
| Le mille ricette di vale (#2) | At least 15 min | **By hand** (≈10 min in a mixer) |
| Vincenzo Capuano TV (#12) | At least 10 min, "with force" | **By hand** |
| Raffaele Vitiello (#5) | ≥10 min until compact | Mixer |
| Mariatta (#6) | ~10 min, then more water | Mixer |
| Pizza Mon Amour (#8) | ~15 min, slow then medium | Mixer |
| Capuano autolisi (#13) | 4–6 min + 7–8 min | Mixer, in two stages |
| Scatti di Gusto home canotto | ~10 min, vigorously | **By hand** |
| La Cucina Ignorante 48 h (#3) | Just to combine, then 1 h rest + 4 fold sets | Minimal knead |
| Wispy Cloud (#17) | Light, then 3 fold sets | Minimal knead |
| Picetto (#18) | ~1 min with a spatula, folds in the fridge | Minimal knead |

From the wider research in this project, Italian home guides put hand kneading at **about 10–20 minutes** for a traditional dough: Rossopomodoro's home method kneads hard on the bench for about 10 minutes; Sfida Pizza gives roughly 20 minutes but stresses that the dough's look matters more than the clock; other guides say 10–15 minutes.

**Bottom line:** for a hand-mixed Neapolitan dough at 60–66%, plan on **15–20 minutes of total kneading**, best split into bursts with rests. Five minutes is too short unless you are deliberately using the fold-heavy minimal-knead method.

## 10.2 Two schools

**School 1 — traditional Naples: one long knead.**
Salt dissolved in water, yeast in, flour rained in until the *punto di pasta*, then a long continuous knead on the bench. Works best at 55–62%, where the dough doesn't stick.

**School 2 — higher hydration: knead in bursts with rests.**
For wetter doughs, rest periods do part of the gluten work, and they stop the dough overheating and turning gluey. One hand-kneading baker uses 5 min knead → 20–30 min rest → 5 min → rest → 5 min → two sets of folds 20 min apart. Cucchiaio d'Argento also recommends short rests between bursts. Scatti di Gusto suggests the same with a mixer: if the dough won't come together, stop 5–10 minutes, flip it, and restart.

**For a 65–68% canotto dough by hand, School 2 is the better fit.** A practical version:

1. Mix to a shaggy mass; rest 15–20 min (autolysis-like).
2. Add salt and reserved water; squeeze through 2–3 min.
3. Knead 10 min.
4. Rest 10 min, covered.
5. Knead 5–10 min until it passes the checks below.
6. 2–3 sets of stretch and folds, 20 min apart.

## 10.3 The hand techniques

**Classic knead.** Push forward with the heel of the hand, fold the dough back over, rotate a quarter turn, repeat. Best once the dough has some structure.

**Slap and fold** (French fold). For sticky dough on a smooth surface (marble, smooth worktop — not wood, which grabs). Lift the dough, slap the bottom onto the counter, stretch the top towards you and fold it over, rotate, repeat. Builds strength fast without adding flour. One recipe at 65% specifies 10–12 minutes by hand this way.

**Stretch and fold** (in the container). Grab one edge, stretch it up until it resists, fold it over the top; rotate a quarter turn; do all four sides. One set takes ~30 seconds. Used during bulk.

**Letter fold / *pieghe a portafoglio*** (on the bench). Stretch the dough into a rectangle, fold in thirds like a letter, rotate, fold in thirds again. Used by Vale (#2) between 30-minute rests.

**Coil fold.** Lift the dough from the middle so the ends tuck under, rotate, repeat. Gentle; good for very wet dough.

**Keep hands wet, not floured.** Adding flour while kneading changes the hydration and leaves raw streaks.

## 10.4 When to stop — readiness checks

The dough is ready when it is:

- **Smooth:** the surface is satin-like, no lumps or torn patches.
- **Elastic:** a poke springs back slowly (not instantly, not not-at-all).
- **Coherent:** it holds a ball shape and comes cleanly off hands and bowl (Sorbillo's test in the madia).
- **Windowpane:** a small piece stretches thin enough to let light through before tearing. At 66% with hand kneading a partial windowpane is fine; the bulk fermentation finishes the development.

Hand-kneaded dough ends up softer and more extensible than machine dough, with a slightly weaker network, so it benefits from a longer bulk to finish developing — which a cold ferment provides.

**Can you over-knead by hand?** Practically no. Over-kneading is a mixer problem (heat and shearing).

## 10.5 Folds — why and how many

- **Purpose:** add strength without heavy kneading, redistribute gas and yeast, even out temperature.
- **When:** in the first 1–2 hours of bulk, every 15–30 minutes.
- **How many:** 2–4 sets. More kneading up front → fewer folds needed. #3 does 4 sets; #16 and #17 do 3; Vale does 2 letter folds.
- **If skipped:** not fatal after a thorough two-stage knead, but the dough has less structure and less even gas distribution. Compensate by balling a bit tighter (section 12).

## 10.6 Dough temperature

Aim for a dough at **23–25 °C** after mixing (STG: ~25 °C; AVPN: ~23 °C). With 20–22 °C flour and room, water at ~20–25 °C gets you there. In summer use cold water; Capuano uses very cold water even in a short dough.

# 11. Fermentation strategy

## 11.1 The three phases

| Phase | Italian | Where | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm start | Puntata (first part) | Room temperature | Wake the yeast, let gluten develop, folds |
| Maturation | Puntata in frigo / maturazione | Fridge 4–6 °C | Flavour, extensibility, digestibility |
| Final proof | Appretto | Room temperature, as balls | Leavening — the rise you'll bake |

## 11.2 How long to keep it warm before the fridge

From the cold-bulk recipes in the compilation:

| Recipe | Warm before fridge | Then fridge |
|---|---|---|
| Vitiello (#5) | 45 min + folds | ≥40 h |
| La Cucina Ignorante 48 h (#3) | ~2 h (1 h rest + 1 h of folds) | 48 h |
| Vale (#2) | ~1.5 h (with folds) | 42 h |
| Sorbillo-style (#15) | ~2 h | ~20 h |
| La Cucina Ignorante 24 h (#4) | 4 h | 24 h |
| Picetto (#18) | ~40 min (in fridge already) | 8–24 h |

**Rule:** 45 min–2.5 h warm before a 40–48 h cold bulk; up to ~4 h before a 24 h cold bulk. Expect at most a 10–20% increase before it goes in.

## 11.3 Fridge temperature

- **Target 4–5 °C** — what most recipes assume (#3 specifies 4 °C; #4 4–6 °C).
- **At 2–3 °C** the dough will ferment much more slowly than planned. Either set the fridge warmer, put the dough on a warmer shelf (door or top, depending on the model), or add a little more yeast or warm-start time.
- **Measure it.** A cheap fridge thermometer in a glass of water on the shelf gives the real number. Dial settings are not temperatures.
- **Middle shelf, away from the back wall** (which can be much colder).
- **Container:** at least double the dough's volume, lightly oiled, airtight lid, tape mark at the starting level.

## 11.4 What to expect after cold bulk

| After 40–48 h cold | Meaning | Staglio timing |
|---|---|---|
| ~1.5× with bubbles on sides and top, slightly domed | On track | Ball 5–6 h before baking |
| 2× or more, many large bubbles, slack | Ahead | Ball later (4 h before); shorter, cooler appretto |
| 1.2–1.3×, dense, few bubbles | Behind (usually a cold fridge or low yeast) | Ball now (6 h+ before); warm appretto at 25–27 °C |
| No change at all | Yeast problem | Test the yeast (9.6); don't count on time to fix it |

## 11.5 Rating fermentation lengths

For a home high-temperature oven and a puffy rim:

| Length | Typical shape | Rating | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 h | Lots of yeast, warm | 3/10 | Tight, bready, yeasty taste, weak rim; emergency only |
| 4 h | Warm (#12) | 5/10 | Rises fine; flat flavour; springy to open |
| 6 h | Warm, 2 h bulk + 4 h balls | 6/10 | Good rise, mild flavour; realistic same-day |
| 8 h | Warm, 2 h + 6 h (STG/AVPN, #11) | 7/10 | The classic Naples day; good flavour; opens easily |
| 12 h | Low yeast warm, or warm start + fridge | 8/10 | Clearly better flavour and stretch |
| 24 h | 2 h warm, ~18 h fridge, 4–6 h balls (#4, #13, #15) | 9/10 | Best balance of flavour, extensibility and risk for home |
| 48 h | 1–2 h warm, ~40 h fridge, 4–6 h balls (#2, #3, #5) | 8–9/10 | A little more flavour; more that can go wrong (flour strength, fridge temperature, yeast dose) |

## 11.6 Schedule templates

**Same-day (6–8 h), room temperature 22–24 °C:**
- T0 mix and knead
- T+0:30 – T+2:30 bulk (bubbles and slight puff by T+1:30)
- T+2:30 staglio
- T+2:30 – T+6:30/8:30 appretto
- Bake when doubled

**24 h (2 h warm + fridge):**
- Day 1, 18:00 mix and knead; folds over the next hour
- Day 1, 20:00 into the fridge (4–5 °C)
- Day 2, 12:00 out; ball straight from the fridge
- Day 2, 12:00 – 17:00 appretto at 22–24 °C
- Day 2, 17:00 bake

**48 h (cold bulk):**
- Day 1, 18:00 mix and knead
- Day 1, 18:30 – 20:00 2–3 fold sets
- Day 1, ~20:00 into the fridge
- Day 2 leave alone
- Day 3, 11:00 – 12:00 ball (5–6 h before baking)
- Day 3, ~14:30 check
- Day 3, 17:00 bake

## 11.7 Speeding up or slowing down

| Need | Do |
|---|---|
| Speed up a proof | Move balls to 25–27 °C: oven off with only the light on (check with a thermometer; crack the door if over ~28 °C), near (not on) a radiator, or a closed box/cold microwave with a mug of just-boiled water refreshed every ~45 min |
| Don't overdo it | Above ~30 °C the surface gets sticky and the gluten weakens |
| Slow down | 30–60 min in the fridge; or a cooler room |
| Hold ready balls | Fridge for up to a few hours; give 30–60 min out before opening |
| Prevent a disaster | If proofing in the oven, set a phone reminder to take the balls out before preheating anything |

# 12. Staglio and pirlatura (balling)

## 12.1 When

- **Bulk-first schedules: 4–6 h before baking** (compilation guidance and #2–#5).
- **Weaker or under-fermented dough: ball later rather than earlier.** A Pizza.it forum pizzaiolo's rule: the weaker the dough, the later you can and must ball it. If balled too early, the balls lose their *nervo* (tension) by bake time. If balled too late, the gluten is too tight.
- **Straight from the fridge is fine.** Cold dough holds tension better and is easier to shape. If very cold (2–3 °C), 20–30 min on the counter first takes the stiffness off without costing much time.

## 12.2 Equipment

Clean dry counter; dough scraper; scale; lightly oiled containers with lids (or a covered box); a small bowl of water for fingertips; a few drops of oil for the counter at 65%+.

## 12.3 Step by step

1. **Release the dough.** Wet the scraper, run it round the container wall, turn the container over and let the dough fall by its own weight. The smooth top ends up underneath — that becomes the outside of the balls.
2. **Cut a strip.** One clean push of the scraper, 6–7 cm wide. Cut, don't tear (La Cucina Ignorante: cutting preserves the alveolatura).
3. **Weigh pieces.** 270 g each (canotto 260–270 g; classic 250 g). Patch small top-ups onto the sticky side; trim if heavy.
4. **Fold.** Smooth side down in the palm. Pull an edge out and fold it to the centre; go round 5–6 times, like closing a pouch. Pinch the centre closed.
5. **Pirlatura.** Flip seam-side down. Cup both hands around the ball and drag it towards you a few centimetres so the bottom grips and the top stretches; quarter turn; repeat 4–6 times. Cucchiaio d'Argento's cue: trace the letter "C" — the dough wraps on itself and you feel it gain tension.
6. **Stop signal.** The moment the surface starts to tear or turns shiny-thin, stop. On weaker dough the tear point comes sooner.
7. **Seal.** Pinch the seam underneath once more.
8. **Box.** Seam down, at least 5 cm apart (they spread), airtight cover. Label with the time.
9. **Optional rest.** 20–30 min on the bench before moving to a warm spot lets the skin relax.

## 12.4 Surface: flour or oil

Davide Esposito (Pizza Napoletana DOC) gives a simple rule: **flour the bench below 65% hydration; use a film of oil above 65%.** Most contemporary doughs are around 70%, so oil is the norm. The counter needs *some* grip to tighten the ball, so keep the oil to a thin film.

## 12.5 How tight

- **Normal, well-risen, strong dough:** moderate tension; your usual fold-rotate-fold 3–5 times is right.
- **Under-risen or slightly weak dough (e.g. skipped folds, cold fridge):** go tighter, until taut like a drum skin but before tearing. The tension supplies structure and traps gas so the ball rises up rather than spreading flat.
- **Verace look:** La Cucina Ignorante and Brigandi prefer simply closing the balls without heavy folding, to avoid trapped large bubbles that scorch.

## 12.6 Containers

- Individual round containers or a shared box with space between balls.
- Narrow containers make balls rise up rather than out (#3).
- Lightly oiled (or dusted with semola, as Cucchiaio d'Argento does in bowls).
- Airtight: a dry skin cracks when opened and won't puff.

# 13. Appretto (the final proof)

## 13.1 Times from the sources

| Source | Appretto |
|---|---|
| STG | 4–6 h at room temperature, use within 6 h |
| AVPN (#1) | 4–6 h |
| Misya (summarising the disciplinare) | ~6 h |
| Vale (#2) | 3–4 h (summer/winter) |
| La Cucina Ignorante (#3, #4) | 3–5 h / 4–6 h |
| Vitiello (#5) | 4–5 h (max 3 h in summer) |
| Pizza Mon Amour (#8) | 5 h |
| Sorbillo (#11) | ~8 h |
| Capuano (#12) | ~2 h |
| Capuano autolisi (#13) | ~6 h at 20 °C |
| Scatti di Gusto home canotto | 7–8 h in a cool place |

## 13.2 Ready signs

- **Roughly doubled** in volume (compilation: 1.5–2× at minimum).
- **Relaxed:** the ball has spread a little and lost its tight dome.
- **Poke test:** a floured fingertip pressed ~1 cm in leaves a dent that **fills back slowly** and incompletely.
  - Springs back immediately → under-proofed; wait.
  - Stays as a hole, or the ball sighs and deflates → over-proofed; bake now, handle very gently.
- **Bubbles** visible under the skin, especially at the base through a clear container.
- **Light:** the ball feels airy when the box is moved.

## 13.3 Temperature

- 22–24 °C is normal.
- 25–27 °C to catch up a slow dough; check every 1–1.5 h.
- Never above ~30 °C.

## 13.4 Checkpoints

- **Halfway (e.g. 2.5–3 h before baking):** if already ~1.5× and soft, move somewhere cooler; if barely moving, move somewhere warmer.
- **30–45 min before baking:** final poke test; light the oven based on the dough, not just the clock.
- **If not ready at bake time:** give it 20–30 more minutes somewhere warm. The oven can wait; an under-proofed ball cannot be fixed in the oven.

## 13.5 Taking balls from a cold second rest

If balls were refrigerated after staglio (#6, #17), give them 30 minutes to 3 hours out before opening, depending on how risen they are. Never open a fridge-cold ball — it tears and snaps back.

# 14. Opening the dough for a canotto rim

## 14.1 The principle

A canotto rim is gas that you **move** into the edge and then **don't touch.** Every step of opening either pushes gas outward from the centre (good) or flattens the rim (bad). The Scatti di Gusto home canotto method describes exactly this: press with the fingertips from the centre outward so the gas collects in the edge, then widen the disc with a rotating motion without crushing the rim.

## 14.2 Step by step

1. **Lift the ball with a scraper** (Pizza Mon Amour uses a steel spatula), keeping it intact, and flip it top-side down into a bowl or onto a bed of **semola rimacinata**. Coat both sides. Semola doesn't burn or absorb into the dough the way 00 flour does.
2. **Place it top-side up** on a semola-dusted counter.
3. **Press from the centre** with flat fingertips, working outward in a spiral, **stopping 2.5–3 cm from the edge.** Never press that outer ring.
4. **Flip** and repeat lightly.
5. **Slap-stretch or drape:** lift the disc and drape it over the backs of your hands (knuckles), letting gravity stretch it as you rotate. Or the Neapolitan *schiaffo* (slap): pass it from hand to hand on its edge.
6. **Target size:** 28–30 cm from a 270 g ball (canotto max ~31 cm; STG max 35 cm).
7. **Centre thickness:** around 0.25–0.4 cm (STG maximum 0.4 cm).
8. **Check for thin spots** against the light; patch by pinching if needed.

## 14.3 Common mistakes

| Mistake | Result | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Pressing all the way to the edge | Flat rim | Leave 2.5–3 cm untouched |
| Using a rolling pin | No rim at all | Hands only (STG rule too) |
| Opening a cold ball | Tears, springs back | Let it warm 30+ min |
| Too much flour under it | Burnt dusty base | Semola, and shake off the excess |
| Under-proofed ball | Fights back, small, dense | More proof time |
| Over-proofed ball | Tears, thin spots, rim collapses | Very gentle handling, bake quickly |
| Toppings to the edge | Sauce pushes the rim down | Keep sauce a finger's width from the rim |

## 14.4 Launching

- Use a **lightly dusted peel** (semola, a little flour). Too much and it burns on the stone; too little and it sticks.
- **Build the pizza on the peel** (or slide it on just before topping) and shake it to check it moves **before** the last topping goes on.
- **Launch with a quick forward-then-back jerk,** placing the far edge on the stone first and pulling the peel out briskly.
- A lightly topped pizza launches far more easily than a loaded one; at 66%+ keep toppings modest and drained.

# 15. Baking on the Witt Fermo

## 15.1 What the oven does

From the Expert Reviews test and the earlier research:

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Fuel | Gas only (ships with a butane regulator; propane permitted) |
| Burner | U-shaped, runs round three sides — more even than an L-burner |
| Stone | Fixed (non-rotating) pizza stone |
| Capacity | 16 in (~40 cm) pizza |
| Claimed heat-up | Stone to 500 °C in ~18 min at 20 °C, no wind |
| Measured heat-up | The reviewer saw 450 °C in 18 min on a 20 °C day; 500 °C on warmer days |
| Thermometer | None built in — use an infrared thermometer |
| Bake time | Claimed under 90 s; the reviewer's thin pizzas took about 60 s |
| Rotation | Turn regularly; buy a turning peel |

## 15.2 Target temperatures

| Zone | Target | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Stone (centre) | **430–460 °C** | At 66%+ hydration, a 500 °C stone chars the base and rim before the crumb sets |
| STG wood oven reference | hearth ~485 °C, vault ~430 °C | Wood ovens have a much hotter dome relative to the floor than a gas oven |
| AVPN reference | 430–480 °C | |

The point of not going to 500 °C: a canotto rim is thick and wet. It needs 60–90 seconds for the inside to set. At 500 °C the outside chars in 40 seconds while the inside stays gummy.

## 15.3 Bake sequence

1. **Preheat 30+ minutes** with the flame high. The air heats in about 10 minutes; the stone takes longer.
2. **Measure the stone** in the centre with an infrared thermometer. Aim for 430–460 °C.
3. **Turn the flame to low** just before launching.
4. **Launch** onto the centre of the stone.
5. **25–30 s:** the side facing the burner rises and browns first.
6. **Turn 180°** with a turning peel.
7. **25–40 s more,** until the rim is leopard-spotted and the base is firm.
8. **Optional:** lift the pizza towards the flame for 3–5 seconds ("doming") to finish the top.
9. **Out,** rest 30 seconds before cutting so steam doesn't make the centre soggy.
10. **Flame back to high;** let the stone recover **1–2 minutes** between pizzas. Re-measure every few pizzas.

Throughput: about **3 minutes per pizza** including recovery, so 12 pizzas take 35–40 minutes. The oven, not the dough, is the bottleneck — which is why the party dough was split into two bowls balled 30 minutes apart.

## 15.4 Reading the bake

| Observation | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Rim burnt, base pale | Flame too high, stone not hot enough | Longer preheat; lower flame at launch |
| Base burnt, top raw | Stone too hot relative to the flame | Let the stone cool to ~430 °C; flame higher; dome the pizza |
| Centre gummy, "more cooked than baked" | Under-proofed or gas-less dough; too much wet topping; too-short bake | Proof longer / check yeast; drain toppings; bake a few seconds longer at a slightly lower temperature |
| Rim pale and dense | Under-proofed, too cold a bake, or the rim was pressed | Proof more; hotter stone; don't touch the rim |
| Big blisters that burn black | Large trapped bubbles from heavy folding at balling | Close balls gently; pop large bubbles before launching |

# 16. Troubleshooting and diagnosis

## 16.1 Dough stage

| Symptom | Likely cause | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Too sticky to knead | Hydration too high for hand work; early in kneading | Wet hands; slap and fold; rest 10 min and continue; next time drop 2% water |
| Won't come together | Gluten not formed yet | Rest 10–15 min and resume; La Cucina Ignorante rests it in the fridge for 15 min |
| Tears when stretched during kneading | Not yet developed | Keep kneading or rest and fold |
| Dough very warm after mixing | Warm water, long kneading, warm room | Use cooler water; rest in a cooler place |

## 16.2 Fermentation stage

| Symptom | Likely cause | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Barely grew in the fridge (1.2–1.3×) | Fridge 2–3 °C; low yeast; long cold | Ball now; warm appretto at 25–27 °C; give it full time |
| Doubled or more in the fridge | Fridge too warm; too much yeast | Ball later; shorter, cooler appretto |
| No growth at all, even warm | Dead or very weak yeast | Test the yeast; restart |
| Sour, alcoholic smell; slack, wet surface | Over-fermented | Ball gently and bake soon; use for focaccia if far gone |

## 16.3 Balls and opening

| Symptom | Likely cause | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Ball spreads flat in the box | Under-tensioned; over-proofed; weak flour | Tighter pirlatura; narrower containers; stronger flour |
| Snaps back when opened | Too cold; under-proofed; flour too strong for the time | Rest longer at room temperature; less Manitoba for short ferments |
| Tears when opened | Over-proofed; too thin; weak flour | Gentler opening; leave the centre a touch thicker |
| Sticks to the peel | Too little semola; wet dough; slow to launch | More semola; top quickly; shake-test before the last topping |

## 16.4 Finished pizza

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Dense, gummy, "more cooked than baked," no rim | Little or no gas in the dough: dead/weak yeast or severely under-proofed | Test yeast; proper dose; proof until doubled |
| Flat but cooked through | Under-proofed or rim pressed | Longer proof; leave the rim alone |
| Soggy centre | Wet sauce or mozzarella | Drain mozzarella; thicker sauce; less of it |
| Bitter burnt base | Too much flour on the peel; stone too hot | Semola; lower stone temperature |
| Bland taste | Very short ferment; low salt | Longer cold maturation; 2.8% salt |

## 16.5 A diagnostic order

When a dough fails, check in this order — the earlier items explain the most failures:

1. **Was the yeast alive and properly measured?** (Proof test; stirred solution or direct weighing.)
2. **Was the dose right for the schedule?** (Compare with the table in 9.3.)
3. **What was the real fridge temperature?** (Thermometer, not dial.)
4. **Was the dough given enough warm time at the end?** (Doubled, poke fills slowly.)
5. **Was the flour strong enough for the duration?** (Section 6.2.)
6. **Was the gluten developed?** (Kneading time, folds, windowpane.)
7. **Was it opened without pressing the rim?**
8. **Was the stone in the right range?**

| Water | 65–66% (64% if too sticky) |
| Salt | 2.8% |
| Yeast | by schedule (table 17.6) |
| Ball weight | 270 g |

## 17.2 Recipe A — 48 h canotto (cold bulk), the party dough corrected

**12 balls (two bowls of 6):**

| Ingredient | Total | Per bowl (6 balls) |
|---|---|---|
| Caputo Pizzeria | 1,440 g | 720 g |
| Manitoba (W ~380) | 480 g | 240 g |
| Water (20–22 °C) | 1,267 g (66%) | 634 g |
| Salt | 54 g | 27 g |
| Fresh yeast | **6 g (0.30%)** | 3 g |
| *or* instant dry yeast | **2.0 g (0.10%)** | 1.0 g |

**3 balls:** 360 g Caputo Pizzeria + 120 g Manitoba, 317 g water, 13 g salt, **1.5 g fresh yeast** or **0.5 g instant** (weigh on a 0.01 g scale, or use a stirred solution).

What changed from the original party plan: yeast raised from 0.05% instant (0.15% fresh) to **0.10% instant (0.30% fresh)**, the middle of the comparable recipes (Vitiello, La Cucina Ignorante, Vale); the yeast is weighed and whisked into the flour rather than portioned from a solution; and the fridge is verified at 4–5 °C.

**Method:**

1. **Weigh and mix dry.** Whisk both flours together. If using instant yeast, whisk it in now. If using fresh, crumble it into the water and stir to dissolve.
2. **Mix.** Pour in the water minus 30 g per bowl. Mix by hand until no dry flour remains. Cover, rest 15–20 min.
3. **Salt.** Sprinkle the salt over the dough, add the reserved 30 g water in splashes, squeeze through with your fingers 2–3 min until no grains can be felt.
4. **Knead 10 min** (slap and fold while sticky, then push-fold-turn). Wet hands, no flour.
5. **Rest 10 min.**
6. **Knead 5–10 min** until smooth, elastic and a poke springs back slowly.
7. **Bulk, warm:** into an oiled container at least twice its volume; 2 sets of stretch and folds, 20–30 min apart; about 1.5–2 h at 22 °C in total.
8. **Fridge 40–44 h** at 4–5 °C (measured), lid on, tape at the dough level.
9. **Staglio 5–6 h before baking** (bowl B 30 min after bowl A). Expect ~1.5×.
10. **Appretto** at 22–24 °C until doubled, relaxed, poke fills back slowly. Check at the halfway point.
11. **Oven** on 45 min before baking; stone at 430–460 °C.
12. **Open** on semola, rim untouched, 28–30 cm.
13. **Bake** 25–30 s, turn 180°, 25–40 s more; 1–2 min recovery between pizzas.

## 17.6 Yeast by schedule (summary)

| Schedule | Fresh | Instant |
|---|---|---|
| Same day, 4 h | 0.3–0.5% | 0.1–0.17% |
| Same day, 6–8 h | 0.2–0.4% | 0.07–0.13% |
| 24 h (2 h warm + ~18 h cold + 5 h balls) | 0.2–0.25% | 0.07–0.08% |
| 36 h | 0.25–0.3% | 0.08–0.1% |
| 48 h (1.5–2 h warm + ~40 h cold + 5–6 h balls) | 0.3% | 0.1% |
| Fridge colder than 4 °C | add ~25–30% | add ~25–30% |
| Summer kitchen (26 °C+) | subtract ~30% | subtract ~30% |

## 17.7 The non-negotiables

1. **Test any yeast you're not sure of.** Ten minutes saves a party.
2. **Weigh yeast directly**, ideally on a 0.01 g scale; if you must use a solution, stir before every portion.
3. **Measure the fridge.** 4–5 °C is what the recipes assume.
4. **Knead properly by hand:** ~15–20 min in total, split by a rest.
5. **Ball 4–6 h before baking**, later for weaker dough.
6. **Proof until doubled and relaxed** — then bake, not before.
7. **Stone at 430–460 °C**, measured.
8. **Never press the rim.**

## 20.3 Techniques referenced by name

- **Bassinage** — French term for adding the last water late, after gluten forms.
